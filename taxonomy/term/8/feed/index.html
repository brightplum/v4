Can you say what it bought?</p> <p>Most directors I talk to can't. It isn't because they don't pay attention. It's because nothing arrives that would tell them. The work happens somewhere out of sight. The only signal comes at the end of the year, when somebody mentions the hours are nearly gone.</p> <p>So here's a month, week by week. You can hold it against the one you're getting now.</p> <h3>Week one: table setting</h3> <p>This is the week that decides whether the other three are any good.</p> <p>We look at the budget for the month. We look at what carried over: work left unfinished, work still in progress, anything that got deferred and shouldn't get deferred again. We figure out what needs to get done before the next check-in with you. Then the team meets internally and plans the month.</p> <h3>Weeks two and three: the work</h3> <p>These are the heavy weeks. They move depending on when your meeting falls.</p> <p>The work splits two ways. There's proactive work, which runs against the six dimensions we track on every site: security, performance, accessibility, documentation, communication, and platform health. Some of that needs your approval before we start. We bring it to you rather than doing it quietly and billing for it.</p> <p>Two things worth separating because vendors blur them. The audit is what we run on a schedule. The remediation is what it costs to act on what the audit found.</p> <p>Security runs monthly. The scan is quick and the fix is usually bumping packages to versions that aren't vulnerable.</p> <p>Accessibility runs quarterly because the audit is cheap. The fix, however, isn't. One scan can surface months of work. None of it is a version bump. That's the line item typically left out of a budget.</p> <p>For almost all of our clients, we've inherited their site. We go slower at the start. We don't know yet how it's meant to work, and often nobody still at the institution does either. So we ask questions and build automated tests. Changing something you didn't know was important is worse than leaving it alone another month.</p> <p>Then there's what you asked for: a feature, a bug, support for your web team. When those come in, they get prioritized in the same week, and every task gets an estimate and a due date.</p> <p>The client meeting lands in here. We bring the site report.</p> <h3>Week four: looking back, and deciding together</h3> <p>Finish what's open, then reflect on it with you.</p> <p>Sometimes work needs doing and the budget won't reach. We borrow ahead against next month, and we get your approval in writing before we do. You should never find out after the fact that a month ran long.</p> <p>Sometimes you tell us it isn't that important and it can wait. Good. It moves into next month's planning and we refocus on something else.</p> <p>Most months it's simpler than either. The work is going to land, the budget is going to hold, and we'll have a fuller update for you next month.</p> <p>The collaboration is the part most vendors skip. They plan, they ask you for a priority, they execute. Then the month ends and nobody comes back to ask how it went.</p> <p>That's the week that makes the next one work. It also produces something that compounds: a record, month by month, of what the site needed and what got decided. A year of those is a budget case you didn't have to write.</p> <h3>What arrives without you asking for it</h3> <p>The site report.</p> <p>It's built to be forwarded. Your team can read it and so can your board. It shows what we did, where the site stands on page speed and what changed in your analytics, and what we recommend next.</p> <blockquote class="pull-quote"><p>You approve a number every month. Can you say what it bought?</p></blockquote> <p>Past, present, and what's coming. That third part is the one that matters, because the reason you need this isn't curiosity about last month. It's that budget renewal is coming, and you should be making that decision with information rather than a feeling.</p> <h3>The month when nothing breaks</h3> <p>This is the month people struggle to justify, and it's the one you're actually paying for.</p> <p>"Nothing broke" looks backward. The report looks forward, and in a quiet month it carries more, not less.</p> <p>Nothing broke, but a security advisory is coming. That goes in the report.</p> <p>Nothing broke, but we ran an accessibility audit and found pages that need remediation. That goes in the report.</p> <p>Nothing broke, but the analytics show a group of visitors having a slow experience. We open a ticket and ask for approval to work on page speed.</p> <p>Nothing broke, and you're current on the things that come first. Then we recommend looking at how people use the site.</p> <p>That one isn't standard. It's a recommendation, and it competes for the same budget as everything else. If you're behind on versions or a compliance deadline is closing, that work goes first and this waits. But when the question is "nothing broke, what now," this is usually the answer.</p> <p>Performance isn't only load time. It's whether a visitor finds what they came for, whether the language and spacing hold together, whether a page needs less on it rather than more.</p> <p>We look at your authors the same way. What are they trying to get done, and how many steps does it take? Sometimes the finding has nothing to do with visitors. Your site works fine for the public and your team is making the same change in four places to publish one thing. Nobody designed that. It accumulated, and it comes straight out of the time your team doesn't have.</p> <h3>What you get from most vendors instead</h3> <p>A report of what was done, if you get one.</p> <p>Nothing arrives until late in the year, and then it's one of two messages. You haven't used your hours, here's what's left. Or the thing you're asking for is outside your hours, here's what it would take.</p> <p>Both arrive too late to do anything with.</p> <p>We forecast instead. Against the budget you already approved, not against a new proposal. And we listen in the monthly check-in for what your team needs. Where are you spending the most time? What would give you time back? The answer to that usually points at the work worth doing next.</p> <h3>What a recommendation should sound like</h3> <p>We don't recommend perfection. We recommend a balance between budget, quality, experience, speed, and priority.</p> <p>Say performance matters to you, and a few pages are heavy. Those pages don't get much traffic and they hold a lot of content. We could spend real budget rebuilding them properly. Instead we cache them harder, which costs a fraction, and put the rest of the budget into the authoring experience your team touches every day.</p> <p>The second one is worth more to you. It just doesn't look as impressive on a list of completed work.</p> <p>Sometimes you'll tell us your priority goes first and ours waits. That's yours to decide. Our job is to make sure you're deciding it with the risk in front of you, in writing, rather than finding out later. The budget is the hard line, not our list of recommendations.</p> <p>If your vendor's recommendations always cost more than your budget, they aren't recommendations. They're a sales pipeline.</p> <h3>What to ask your vendor this week</h3> <p>Tell me what's coming.</p> <p>Not what you did. What's ahead in the next six to twelve months, and whether the budget I've already approved covers it.</p> <p>Then ask the one question that separates a proactive partner from a responsive one: how will you use the rest of my budget to give my team time back?</p> <p>Your list of sites is going to keep growing. Your headcount isn't. A partner who only answers requests is adding to your workload, one ticket at a time. A partner worth keeping is subtracting from it.</p> <p>If they can't answer that inside the money you've already spent, you have your answer.</p></div> <div> <div><a href="https://brightplum.com/taxonomy/term/8" hreflang="en">Higher Education</a></div> </div> Wed, 23 Sep 2026 18:43:10 +0000 admin 40 at https://brightplum.com What it costs to maintain a university website for a year https://brightplum.com/blog/what-it-costs-maintain-university-website-year <span>What it costs to maintain a university website for a year</span> <div><div> <div> <div class="visually-hidden">Image</div> <div> <img loading="lazy" src="https://brightplum.com/sites/default/files/styles/large/public/2026-09/blog-university-website-budget.webp?itok=LdUHzP0y" width="480" height="270" alt="A hand with a pen reviewing a printed monthly budget report with charts"> </div> </div> </div> </div> <span><span>admin</span></span> <span><time datetime="2026-09-14T12:09:09-05:00" title="Monday, September 14, 2026 - 12:09">Mon, 09/14/2026 - 12:09</time> </span> <div><p><em>By Fredric, Principal at Bright Plum</em></p><p>Every budget season, a client asks me some version of this. How much should we set aside for the website next year?</p><p>It's a hard question to answer well. I've been in tech almost twenty years, most of it working with colleges and universities, so I know what's riding on the number. You have to walk into a room and defend it. Get it wrong and you either run out of money in March or you hand back budget you'll never see again.</p><p>The real answer has a lot of factors in it. But a convoluted answer isn't an answer. "It depends" is what you get from most vendors, and it leaves you where you started.</p><p>Somebody is going to ask you for this number in April. You should already have one.</p><p>So here's the answer. $45,000 a year for a primary university website, maintained the way it should be. Add about $10,000 for each additional property you own if they share the same codebase.</p><p>That's not an industry survey. It's our own book of higher-ed clients. Here's how it breaks down.</p><p>$30,000 is the minimum. At $30,000 you're covering security and the basics of accessibility and compliance, and that's all. No documentation. No performance work. No monitoring for search. Nothing set aside for what's coming next year.</p><p>$45,000 basically buys the rest: documentation, platform health, page speed, and monitoring for both search engines and answer engines. Answer engine optimization (AEO) is the newer half of that. It's the work of making sure your programs come up when a prospective student asks ChatGPT about them instead of typing a search query.</p><p>The rest of this is how you get from that range to your number.</p><h3>First: what did you spend last year?</h3><p>This can feel like a sales question. It isn't meant to be. It's the fastest way to a number you can defend.</p><p>I had a client ask what they should budget for the website this year. They had close to eight Drupal and WordPress sites. The Wordpress sites shared one codebase and the Drupal sites had two different codebases. In the question, all of it had collapsed into "the website."</p><p>So I went back to what they spent with us the prior year. Round number, $100,000. Then I told them what it covered: eight sites. Divide it evenly and that's $12,500 per site per year.</p><p>The money never split evenly, though. The main site carries more visibility and more weight than a department site nobody has touched since 2019, but still needs to be maintained. When I looked at the real breakdown, about 40% went to the most visible site and 60% went to the other seven.</p><p>That puts the main site at $40,000, right where I said it would be. The other $60,000 is the cost of seven sites nobody counted when they said "the website."</p><blockquote class="pull-quote"><p>Somebody is going to ask you for this number in April. You should already have one.</p></blockquote><p>That's the conversation you can't have until you know what you spent. It will change what you ask for. "We need $100,000 for the website" is a number your VP will push back on. "We support eight sites, the main one takes 40% of the effort, and here's what that bought" is a number your VP can approve.</p><p>If you just finished a rebuild or a rebrand, your last-year number will be large, and that's still useful. Ask for the line-item breakdown and sort it into two piles.</p><p>One pile is one-time work. Content migration and content strategy usually belong here. You paid for them once. You won't pay for them again next year, so take them out of the baseline.</p><p>The other pile is repeatable. Back-end development, front-end development, component development. As your authors and your users keep working in the site, that work keeps coming. It stays in the baseline.</p><p>Most of the sticker shock in budget season comes from leaving one-time costs in the recurring number.</p><h3>Second: what's coming this year?</h3><p>Once you have a baseline, look forward. These are the things we tell clients to plan for.</p><p>Accessibility audits, at least quarterly, plus the remediation that comes out of them. The audit is the cheap part. Fixing what it finds is the line item people forget.</p><p>Security review, at least monthly, plus remediation. That means hardening packages and checking whether any modules or front-end packages need to be deprecated because of a known issue.</p><p>Major version releases. If a new major version of Drupal or WordPress is projected to land in your fiscal year, set money aside now.</p><p>Documentation of how your site works. Most higher-ed teams run on knowledge that lives in one person's head, and that person is going to leave.</p><p>Platform health and performance.</p><p>Search and answer engine monitoring, if you have goals there.</p><p>Here's the math. Start at the $30,000 floor. Add 25% to 40% of it for the forward-looking work on that list and you're at $38,000 to $42,000. Add the third question below and you land at $45,000. That isn't a coincidence. That's where the number comes from.</p><p>That percentage is money spent before anything breaks. It's the least popular line item to defend and the one that decides whether your year is calm or on fire.</p><h3>Third: what are you trying to accomplish?</h3><p>The first two questions get you a number for keeping the site alive. They don't cover what you're trying to do with it.</p><p>If you have growth goals or engagement goals, those cost money. If you have board meetings and accreditation reviews, somebody has to prepare for them, and somebody has to make sure your VP walks in with the materials they need.</p><p>We don't write content. What we can do is monitor, make suggestions, and hand your content people prompts and ideas they can act on. Interview a few current students. Interview prospective families. Turn what comes out of that into prompts your writers can use, especially if you care about showing up in answer engines.</p><p>That work belongs in the budget too, and it's usually the first thing cut, because it's the hardest to point at.</p><h3>What to do before your budget meeting</h3><p>Budget decisions at most institutions land in April for the next fiscal year. The work that gets you a good number happens well before that.</p><p>Pull last year's invoices. Count how many sites that money covered. Split the line items into one-time and repeatable, and drop the one-time work out of your baseline. Then add 25% to 40% for what's coming.</p><p>You can do all of that without calling anyone.</p><hr><p><em>Fredric runs Bright Plum.
